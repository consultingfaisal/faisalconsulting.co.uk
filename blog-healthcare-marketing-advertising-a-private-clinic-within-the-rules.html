Healthcare marketing: advertising a private clinic within the rules

20 November 2026

Advertising a private clinic is not like advertising a shop. The rules are tighter, the regulators take complaints seriously, and an ad can break the rules without naming a single product.

Many clinics react by doing very little marketing at all. That is a cost too. The better answer is to know where the lines are, so the team can advertise with confidence.

This guide covers the rules that most often catch clinics out in the UK. It is not legal advice. For anything you are unsure about, the ASA's Copy Advice team and your own legal adviser are the right people to ask.

Three sets of rules apply at once

Most clinic marketing has to pass three separate tests. It helps to keep them apart in your head, because each one is checked by a different body.

Medicines themselves are regulated by the MHRA, and its Blue Guide covers how medicines may be advertised. The CAP Code points marketers to it.

  • The CAP Code, enforced by the ASA. Section 12 covers medicines, medical devices, treatments, health-related products and beauty products. It applies to your ads, social posts and website claims.
  • Google Ads policies. Google has its own healthcare and medicines policy and its own targeting rules. An ad can be legal in the UK and still be limited or disapproved by Google.
  • Data protection law, overseen by the ICO. Anything a patient tells you through an enquiry form is likely to be health data.

Prescription-only medicines cannot be advertised to the public

CAP Code rule 12.12 is short. Prescription-only medicines and prescription-only medical treatments may not be advertised to the public. This is the rule clinics break most often.

The ASA's advice says it considers almost every reference to a prescription-only medicine to be a promotion of it. Naming the product is the obvious breach. But a reference can also be implied, through a description, an image of an injection pen, or a mix of both.

The ASA's advice names Botox, weight management medicines including GLP-1 treatments, and erectile dysfunction treatments among the common examples. It applies to Google search ads, social media ads, posts and hashtags.

Landing pages count too. The ASA has ruled against an ad that did not mention a prescription-only medicine itself, because it linked straight to a page that did.

Promote the consultation, not the product

This does not mean a clinic cannot advertise at all. The ASA's advice is to promote the consultation rather than the medicine.

Its own examples are a consultation for the treatment of lines and wrinkles, or a consultation for the treatment of erectile dysfunction. These are likely to be acceptable, as long as the medicine itself is not referred to directly or indirectly.

In practice, that means checking every part of the ad: the headline, the image, the price offer, the hashtags and the page it lands on. One stray reference can turn a compliant consultation ad into an ad for a prescription-only medicine.

Every claim needs evidence

Rule 12.1 says objective claims must be backed by evidence, where relevant from trials conducted on people. If you say a treatment reduces pain or improves results, you need to be able to prove it.

Several other rules in section 12 come up again and again in clinic advertising.

  • Do not claim a treatment is guaranteed to work, absolutely safe or without side effects unless you hold proof (rule 12.9).
  • Unqualified claims such as cure or rejuvenation are not generally acceptable (rule 12.7).
  • Do not suggest something is safe or effective just because it is natural (rule 12.10).
  • Do not discourage essential treatment for conditions that need medical supervision (rule 12.2).
  • Practitioners must have relevant and recognised qualifications, and you should encourage people to take independent medical advice before significant or invasive treatments (rule 12.3).

Before and after photos and endorsements

Before and after photos are powerful, which is why the ASA watches them closely. It treats them in the same way as testimonials.

That means holding signed and dated proof that the photos are genuine and have not been manipulated. The photos must not exaggerate what the treatment can achieve, and you need evidence for the impression they give.

For prescription-only medicines, the ASA's advice is to avoid before and after images altogether, because they are likely to be seen as an advertising claim for the medicine.

Endorsements need the same care. Rule 12.18 says marketers must not use health professionals or celebrities to endorse medicines. The ASA has treated a blogger with around 30,000 followers as a celebrity for this purpose, so influencer posts are not a way round the rule.

Cosmetic treatments and under 18s

Rule 12.25 says ads for cosmetic interventions must not be directed at people under 18, through the choice of media or the context the ad appears in. The CAP Code defines cosmetic interventions widely. It includes surgical and non-surgical procedures, invasive or not, where the main aim is to change appearance.

So check where your ads appear, not only what they say. Social platforms, influencers and media with a young audience all need thought.

CAP's guidance on cosmetic procedures also covers how practitioners describe themselves. It sets out when terms such as surgeon, cosmetic surgeon, specialist and consultant are acceptable. Read it before you write a practitioner biography.

Google Ads has its own healthcare policy

Passing the CAP Code does not mean Google will run your ads. Google's healthcare and medicines policy sits on top of UK law, and some content needs certification first.

If an ad is restricted but allowed in your target locations, Google labels it Eligible (limited). That is normal for parts of healthcare. A disapproval is the one to fix.

  • Prescription drug terms are restricted in ads, landing pages and keywords.
  • In the UK, telemedicine providers must be certified by LegitScript or accredited by NABP, and also certified with Google. They cannot promote prescription drugs in their ads or landing pages.
  • Online pharmacies in the UK must be registered with the General Pharmaceutical Council.
  • Birth control, fertility and STI testing are restricted in certain locations.
  • In the UK, ads using queries about getting an abortion need Google certification first.
  • Speculative and experimental treatments are not allowed.

Remarketing and patient lists are off limits

This is the rule that surprises clinics most. Google treats health as a sensitive interest category in personalised advertising. That covers physical and mental health conditions, and invasive procedures including cosmetic surgery and injections.

If you promote in this category, you cannot use advertiser-curated audiences. That includes Customer Match, your own data segments such as website visitor lists, and lookalike segments. Predefined Google audiences, such as in-market and affinity segments, can still be used.

So a campaign that uploads your patient list or chases recent website visitors around the internet is likely to be disapproved. Build your plan on search, location and predefined audiences instead.

Handle enquiry data as health data

When someone fills in an enquiry form and mentions their condition, that is data concerning health. The ICO lists it as special category data, and says it should be identified and handled carefully, even when it does not feel especially sensitive.

Check what your forms ask for, where the answers are stored, and which tools can see them. Then make sure your privacy notice matches what actually happens.

A short check before any ad goes live

Put these questions to every new ad, post and landing page. They take a few minutes and catch most problems.

  • Does anything name, show or imply a prescription-only medicine, including the landing page?
  • Is every objective claim backed by evidence you could send the ASA today?
  • Are before and after photos genuine, signed, dated and representative?
  • Is any health professional or influencer endorsing a medicine?
  • Could a cosmetic treatment ad reach people under 18?
  • Does the Google campaign use any advertiser-curated audience?
  • Is enquiry data handled as health data?

None of this means a clinic has to stay quiet. It means building a plan around what is allowed, then measuring which activity actually leads to treatment.
