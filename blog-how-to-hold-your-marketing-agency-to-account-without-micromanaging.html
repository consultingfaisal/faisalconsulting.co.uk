How to hold your marketing agency to account without micromanaging

24 November 2026

Most businesses that use a marketing agency end up in one of two places. Either they hear very little until the monthly report arrives, or they check every post, every ad and every change and wonder why progress is slow.

Neither works well. The first leaves you paying for activity you cannot judge. The second takes your time, slows the agency down and blurs who is responsible for the result.

There is a middle way. You hold the agency to account for outcomes, you keep control of the things only you should control, and you leave the day to day work to the people you are paying to do it. This guide sets out how.

Accountability is about outcomes, not tasks

Micromanaging means watching the work. Accountability means agreeing the result and then checking whether it happened.

If you approve every headline, the agency stops owning the outcome. When results are poor, the honest answer becomes that you signed off the work. That is not a position you want to be in.

So start by separating two kinds of decision. Decisions about what marketing is for, how much it can spend and what counts as success belong to you. Decisions about how to get there, such as which keywords to bid on or which creative to test, belong to the agency, within limits you have agreed.

Write down what the agency is for

Many agency relationships run on a proposal written before the work began and a contract that lists services. Neither says clearly what the business expects to get back.

Put it on one page. Keep it short enough that both sides will actually read it again.

Review this page whenever the business changes direction. An agency cannot be held to a goal it was never told about.

  • The business outcome the work should support, such as qualified enquiries, booked appointments or online sales.
  • The one or two measures that show whether that outcome is improving.
  • What is in scope and what is not, so gaps do not fall between you.
  • The budget, and who can change it.
  • Who in your business is the single point of contact, and how quickly they will reply.

Own your accounts and your data

This is the one area where you should not leave things to the agency. If the agency owns the ad account or the analytics property, you may lose your history when the relationship ends.

Google Ads has five access levels: Email only, Billing, Read only, Standard and Admin. Standard users can edit campaigns. Admin users can also give access to others, change access levels and unlink manager accounts. Someone in your business should hold Admin access to your own account. An agency usually needs Standard access to do its job.

Google Analytics 4 works in a similar way. Its roles are Administrator, Editor, Marketer, Analyst and Viewer. Only an Administrator can manage users. Again, the Administrator role should sit with someone in your business.

Google's own security advice is to give each person the minimum access they need, to give each person their own login rather than a shared one, and to remove access for anyone who no longer works on the account. Check the user list every few months. It takes a few minutes.

Agree how results will be measured before you judge them

Arguments about agency performance are often arguments about numbers. The agency points to one figure. The business points to another. Both are reading different reports.

Settle this at the start. Agree which conversions count, where they are recorded and which report is the source of truth. If your Google Ads and GA4 figures do not match, decide which one you will use for decisions and why.

Then check that the tracking works before you hold anyone to a target. A target built on broken tracking is not a fair test for the agency or for you.

Set a rhythm and keep to it

A steady rhythm of reviews replaces the urge to check in every day. When both sides know when the next conversation is, there is less need to chase.

A simple pattern works for most small and medium businesses.

Keep each meeting to the agreed measures and the decisions that follow from them. If the meeting is mostly a walk through of slides, ask for the report in advance and use the time for questions.

  • A short monthly review of results against the agreed measures, with what the agency changed and what it plans next.
  • A longer quarterly review of whether the plan, budget and scope still fit the business.
  • A clear route for anything urgent in between, such as tracking breaking or spend running far ahead of plan.

Ask for reasons, not just reports

A report tells you what happened. Accountability needs to know why, and what the agency did about it.

For each important change in results, ask three questions. What changed? Why do you think it changed? What are you doing about it? A good agency will welcome these questions, because they give it room to show its thinking.

In Google Ads you do not have to rely on memory. Change history lists changes made to the account, campaigns and ad groups over the past two years. For changes made in the Google Ads interface, it shows the email address of the person who made them. You can filter by date, campaign, user and type of change, such as budget or keyword edits. Use it to match changes in results with changes in the account, not to question every edit.

Agree what needs your sign off

Clear decision rights are what stop accountability turning into micromanagement. Write down which changes the agency can make on its own and which need your approval.

Changes that usually need sign off include raising the budget, starting a new channel, changing what counts as a conversion, and anything that changes what you say about prices, guarantees or regulated claims. Day to day changes, such as bids, keywords, audiences and creative tests within the agreed budget, are normally left to the agency.

Then stick to it. If you agreed the agency can test new ads without asking, do not ask to approve each one. If you want more control in one area, change the agreement openly.

Signs you have the balance wrong

It is easy to drift too far one way or the other. These signs help you notice.

If you see signs from both lists, the cause is usually a missing piece from earlier in this guide: an unclear outcome, unclear measures or unclear decision rights.

  • Too loose: you could not say what the agency changed last month, or why.
  • Too loose: nobody in the business has Admin access to the ad or analytics accounts.
  • Too loose: the report shows clicks and impressions but nothing about enquiries or sales.
  • Too tight: the agency waits for your approval before routine changes.
  • Too tight: you spend more time reviewing the work than the outcome.
  • Too tight: the agency has stopped making suggestions.

When results still do not improve

Sometimes the arrangement is clear and fair, and results still do not move. Before you decide to change agency, raise the problem in writing. Say which measure is off target, by how much and since when. Ask the agency for a plan and agree a date to review it.

Check your contract for the notice period and for what happens to accounts, assets and data at the end. If you already own your accounts, changing agency is far less disruptive.

Also ask whether the agency has what it needs from you. Slow approvals, changing priorities and poor lead follow up can hold back good work. Accountability runs both ways.

Holding an agency to account is mostly about setting things up well: a clear outcome, accounts you own, measures you trust, a steady rhythm and agreed decision rights. Once those are in place, you can step back from the daily detail with confidence.
