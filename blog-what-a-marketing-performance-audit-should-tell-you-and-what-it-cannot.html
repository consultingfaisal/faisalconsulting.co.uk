What a marketing performance audit should tell you, and what it cannot

16 November 2026

A marketing performance audit is a look across the whole of your marketing to find where money is being lost. Not just the ads. The tracking, the website, the follow up, the reporting and the people who own each part.

Businesses usually ask for one at a turning point. Results have slipped. A new budget is due. The board wants to know what marketing is really returning. Or a new leader has arrived and wants a clear starting point.

This guide is for the person commissioning the audit. It sets out what a good audit should tell you, what no audit can tell you, and how to prepare so the work answers the questions you actually have.

Whether your numbers can be trusted

The first thing an audit should tell you is whether the figures in your reports mean what you think they mean. Everything else depends on this. If a conversion counts the wrong thing, every cost per lead built on it is wrong as well.

Expect the audit to list what each platform counts as a result, and to check those counts against something the business holds itself. That might be enquiries in the CRM, orders in the shop or invoices raised. If the platforms and the business disagree, the audit should say by how much and whether the gap has changed over time.

A steady gap is often fine. A gap that suddenly widens usually means something in the tracking changed, not the marketing.

  • A list of every conversion or goal, with a plain note of what it records.
  • A comparison of platform figures with your own records for the same period.
  • Any dates when tracking broke, changed or was duplicated.

Where money is being lost, with the evidence

The second thing is where the leaks are. This is the part people expect, but the useful version is specific. It names the campaign, the page, the form or the step in the process, and shows how the audit knows.

A good finding is something you could check yourself. It separates what was seen from what it might mean. A form that sends no confirmation email is a fact. That it is costing you leads is a reasonable guess, and the audit should say so in those words.

Watch for findings that are really opinions. Phrases such as best practice or industry standard are not evidence. Ask what the finding is based on, and what else could explain it.

  • Each finding tied to a source you can open.
  • A clear line between what was observed and what is inferred.
  • Another possible explanation for each finding, so you can judge it fairly.

What to fix first

A list of forty issues is not a result. Most businesses can act on two or three things at once. The audit should put the findings in order and explain the order.

The order usually follows dependency as much as size. If tracking is broken, fixing it comes before any judgement on channels, because you cannot measure an improvement you cannot see. If the website loses people at one step, changing the ads that send people there will do little until that step is fixed.

Ask for the effort and the owner next to each priority. A fix that needs a developer for a month is a different decision from a setting someone can change this afternoon.

The baseline you will measure progress against

An audit should end with a number, or a small set of numbers, that everyone agrees to measure future work against. Without that, nobody can show later whether the changes worked.

The baseline should be something the business trusts, not just a platform figure. Qualified leads per month, cost per sale or revenue from repeat customers are common choices. Write down how each one is calculated and where it comes from, so it can be worked out the same way in six months.

What it cannot tell you: data that was never kept

An audit can only read what was recorded. Some gaps are simply the way the tools work, and it helps to know them before you start.

In Google Analytics 4, standard properties can keep user level and event data for 2 months or 14 months. Google says this setting does not affect the standard aggregated reports, but it does limit explorations and funnel reports. If your property is set to 2 months, a detailed look further back is not possible.

Google Ads keeps change history for the past 2 years. Older changes will not be there. GA4 also withholds some rows when there are too few users, for example in reports that use demographic data. Google says these thresholds are set by the system and cannot be adjusted.

None of this is a fault in the audit. But it means some questions about the past will have no firm answer, and a good audit says so rather than guessing.

  • Check the GA4 data retention setting before the audit starts.
  • Gather your own records of big changes, such as site launches, price changes and agency moves.
  • Expect the audit to name any question it could not answer, and why.

What it cannot tell you: cause, or next year

An audit looks backwards. It can show that leads fell in the same month a campaign changed. It cannot prove the change caused the fall. Seasons, competitors, prices and the wider economy all move at the same time.

So treat the findings about cause as starting points. The useful next step is a small, cheap test that tells you which explanation is right before you commit to a large fix. Turn a setting off in one area and not another. Fix the form and watch the next few weeks.

Nor can an audit forecast what you will get next year. It can tell you where the current spend is weak and what is worth testing. Anyone who promises a fixed return from an audit is guessing.

What it cannot do: make the change for you

Many of the most useful findings are about people and process, not settings. Nobody owns the lead follow up. The agency reports activity, not results. Sales and marketing count leads differently.

An audit can name these clearly. It cannot fix them. That needs someone inside the business with the time and authority to act, and a regular moment when progress is checked against the baseline.

Before the audit, decide who will own the findings. If nobody can, the report will be read once and filed.

How to prepare so the audit is worth it

A little preparation makes the work faster and the result more useful. Start with the questions. Write down the two or three decisions the audit should help you make, such as whether to keep an agency or where next year's budget should go.

Then gather access. Read only access is usually enough to start. Give it to a named person, and remove it when the work ends.

  • The decisions you want the audit to inform.
  • Read only access to analytics, ad platforms, Search Console and the CRM.
  • Your own figures for leads, sales or revenue over the same period.
  • A short timeline of major changes in the last year or two.
  • The name of the person who will own the findings.

A marketing performance audit is worth paying for when it tells you whether to trust your numbers, where money is leaking, what to fix first and what to measure from now on. It is worth less when it promises certainty about the past or the future that the data cannot give.
