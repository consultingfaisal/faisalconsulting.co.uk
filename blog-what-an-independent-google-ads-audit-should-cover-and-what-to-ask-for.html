What an independent Google Ads audit should cover, and what to ask for at the end

14 November 2026

An independent Google Ads audit is a second opinion on where your ad money goes. You might want one because results have dropped, because a new agency is pitching, or because nobody in the business can explain the numbers with confidence.

The trouble is that audit can mean anything. Some are a screenshot of the optimisation score and a list of tips. Others take the account apart and show their working. This guide is for the person buying the audit. It covers what independent should mean, the access to give, what the review should look at, and what you should hold in your hands at the end.

What independent should mean

Independent means the auditor has nothing to gain from a particular answer. Ask about this before you share access. A free audit from an agency that wants to run your account is a sales pitch with a spreadsheet attached. It can still be useful, but read it knowing what the author hopes you will do next.

Ask three plain questions at the start. Who pays for the audit? Will the auditor offer to manage the account afterwards? Do they earn anything from the tools or platforms they recommend? None of these answers rules anyone out. They tell you how to read the findings.

  • Agree in writing that the audit is a diagnosis, not a proposal.
  • Ask whether management work would be offered afterwards, and on what terms.
  • Ask the auditor to say where a finding is opinion rather than evidence.

Give read-only access, and keep ownership

Google Ads has several access levels for users: Email-only, Billing, Read-only, Standard and Admin. Read-only is enough to start most audits. Google's own table shows that a read-only user can view campaigns, use the planning tools and run reports. They cannot edit campaigns, change who has access, or add and remove product links.

Give access to a named person, not a shared login. When the audit ends, remove it. If the auditor later needs to change something to test a finding, agree that change first and upgrade the access for that task only.

While you are in the access settings, look at who else is there. Note any users and managers you do not recognise, and which email address holds Admin. If only your agency holds Admin, ask why, and fix it whatever the audit finds.

  • Invite the auditor as Read-only.
  • Note every user, manager and Admin on the account.
  • Make sure someone inside your business holds Admin.

Start with what the account counts as a result

A good audit looks at measurement before performance. If the account counts the wrong thing, every cost per result in the report is wrong too.

Google Ads splits conversion actions into primary and secondary. Primary actions are reported in the Conversions column and are used for bidding when their goal is used for bidding. Secondary actions appear in the All conversions column but are not used for bidding. A newsletter signup set as primary can therefore steer the bids just as much as a sales enquiry.

Expect the audit to list each conversion action, say what it really records, and say whether it should be primary. It should also compare what Google Ads reports with what the business actually received in the same period, such as enquiries in the CRM or orders in the shop.

  • A list of conversion actions, each marked primary or secondary.
  • A plain note of what each one records.
  • A check of reported conversions against real leads or sales.

Read the account's history, not just today's settings

Today's settings only show where the account has ended up. The history shows how it got there. Change history in Google Ads lists changes from the past two years, such as a paused campaign, a new keyword or an old budget. You can filter it by type of change, date range, campaign or ad group, and the User column shows who made a change in the interface.

Auto-applied recommendations deserve their own check. Google can apply some recommendations for you, and you can turn this on or off at any time. The History tab in the Recommendations section summarises what was applied recently.

An audit should line up big changes with shifts in results. A drop that started the week a bid strategy changed tells a different story from one that started when a form broke.

  • A short timeline of major changes and who made them.
  • A note of any auto-applied recommendations that are switched on.
  • Changes matched against the dates when results moved.

Treat the optimisation score with care

Google describes the optimisation score as an estimate of how well the account is set to perform, shown on a scale up to 100. It is built from Google's recommendations.

Google also says the account can reach the top score by applying or dismissing all recommendations. So the score tells you how many recommendations are still open. It does not tell you whether the account makes money. An audit that leads with the score, or promises to raise it, is measuring the wrong thing.

Ask the auditor to go through the open recommendations one by one and say which ones fit your goals and which ones would spend more without a clear return.

Check the settings that decide who sees the ads

Some settings quietly widen who can see an ad. Location is a common one. The default and recommended option in Google Ads is Presence or interest, which reaches people likely to be in your target area or who regularly are. The other option, Presence, focuses on people located there or regularly located there. Neither is wrong. The audit should say which one each campaign uses and whether that suits a business that serves a fixed area.

Search partners are another. These are sites that partner with Google to show ads. The audit should say whether they are switched on and whether they earn their place.

After the settings, the review should look at where spend went: the search terms, the match types, and the pages the ads send people to. Those checks are covered in more depth in other guides on this site, so the point here is simply that the audit should include them.

  • Location option for each campaign, and whether it fits.
  • Networks each campaign runs on.
  • Search terms, match types and landing pages, in that order.

What to ask for at the end

The deliverable matters more than the meeting. A call where someone talks through slides is easy to forget. A written set of findings can be checked, shared and acted on. Ask for these before you agree to the audit, so the auditor knows the standard.

Each finding should say what was seen, where it was seen, and what it might mean. Keep the observation and the interpretation apart. An auditor who writes the conversion action counts page views is telling you a fact. An auditor who writes the agency is wasting money is giving an opinion, and should show the evidence for it.

  • Findings with the evidence behind each one, such as a report, a date range or a setting.
  • A clear line between what was observed and what it might mean.
  • A statement of what the numbers can and cannot be trusted for today.
  • Two or three priorities in order, not a list of forty tips.
  • The smallest test for each priority, so you can check it before a big change.
  • What the audit did not cover, and why.
  • A baseline figure to measure future work against.
  • Confirmation that the auditor's access has been removed.

A Google Ads audit is worth paying for when it leaves you able to act without the auditor in the room.
