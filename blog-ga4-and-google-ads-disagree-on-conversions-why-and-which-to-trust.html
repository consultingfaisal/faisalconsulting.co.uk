GA4 and Google Ads disagree on conversions: why, and which to trust

22 November 2026

You open Google Ads and it says 40 conversions last month. You open GA4 and it says 31. Someone asks which one is right, and the honest answer is that both can be.

The two products count different things, on different dates, with different rules. A gap on its own does not mean tracking is broken. A gap you cannot explain, or one that suddenly changes size, is worth chasing.

This guide covers the common reasons the numbers differ, how to line the reports up before you compare them, and which number to use for which job. The figures above are only an illustration.

Key events and conversions are not the same thing

Start with the words, because Google changed them. In GA4, an event that measures an important action is now called a key event. A conversion now means an important action you use to measure ad campaigns and to guide bidding.

Google says key events are designed for looking at traffic acquisition, user behaviour and site journeys. Conversions are for measuring advertising return, checking numbers against Google Ads, and media planning. Google also notes that, before this change, events marked as conversions in Analytics were measured differently from Google Ads conversions, and that this caused discrepancies.

So the first question is simple. Are you comparing a GA4 key event with a Google Ads conversion? If so, you are comparing two different measures, and some gap is expected.

The date the conversion lands on

This is the reason most people miss. Google Ads reports a conversion against the date of the click that led to it. Google Analytics reports it on the date it happened.

Google gives a plain example. Someone clicks an ad on 19 July and buys on 20 July. Google Ads puts that sale on 19 July. Analytics puts it on 20 July.

Over a month this mostly evens out. Over a week, or at the edge of a date range, it does not. Conversions also keep arriving for days after the click, so recent days in Google Ads look weak at first and then fill in.

Google Ads has columns that use the conversion date instead, such as All conv. (by conv. time). Google suggests these when you compare with a system that records the date of the conversion.

Attribution model and channel credit

Both products decide which click gets the credit, and they do not always decide the same way. GA4 sets a reporting attribution model in its attribution settings. That model applies to key event reports and explorations that use event scoped dimensions such as source, medium and campaign. Session and first user dimensions are not affected by it.

There is also the question of which channels can take credit. In GA4 reports, paid and organic channels are eligible by default. For conversions shared with Google Ads, the default is Google paid channels only. If an enquiry came from an ad click and then a later organic visit, the two products may credit it differently.

Since November 2023, GA4 no longer offers the first click, linear, time decay and position based models. If a report or a colleague still talks about those, check what the property is actually set to.

Windows, counting and time zones

Several quieter settings move the numbers too. Each one is worth a look before you blame the tracking.

  • Conversion window. A Google Ads conversion window runs from one to 90 days. A sale after the window is not recorded in Google Ads, but Analytics can still record it.
  • Lookback window. In GA4 the default is 90 days for most key events and 30 days for acquisition events such as first_visit. A shorter setting means fewer touchpoints can take credit.
  • Counting method. In Google Ads, Every counts each conversion after a click and One counts a single conversion per click. If someone sends two forms after one click, GA4 records two key events. A Google Ads action set to One records one.
  • Primary and secondary actions. The Conversions column in Google Ads only includes primary actions. All conv. includes secondary actions and view through conversions too.
  • Time zone. GA4 reports in the property time zone and Google Ads in the account time zone. If they differ, conversions near midnight fall on different days.
  • Processing time. Google Ads reporting can lag by 24 to 48 hours, and imported Analytics conversions can take up to 24 hours to appear.

What Google Ads can see that GA4 cannot

Google Ads counts some conversions that never appear in GA4 as a click led session. View through conversions are one. Someone sees an ad, does not click it, and converts later. These sit in the View through conversions and All conversions columns, not in Conversions.

Cross device conversions are another. A click on a phone and a purchase on a laptop can be linked in Google Ads. Google explains that it uses models built from privacy safe data from signed in users to estimate conversions it cannot observe directly. GA4 may show that purchase as a separate visit from another source.

Google also says that some conversions in Google Ads are modelled, where not every conversion can be observed. So the Google Ads figure is partly an estimate by design.

What breaks the link between the two

Some gaps are real faults. The most common is a lost click ID. Auto tagging adds a click identifier, the GCLID, to the landing page address. If a redirect strips it, or the page moves on before the Google tag runs, GA4 cannot tie the visit to the ad.

Google's troubleshooting advice is practical. Add ?gclid=test to your landing page address and see whether it survives to the final page. Make sure the Google tag fires before any redirect. If auto tagging is off and there are no manual campaign tags, ad traffic can show as organic, direct or (not set).

If you import GA4 key events into Google Ads, check how that was set up. Only key events linked to Google Ads campaigns become conversions. Imported actions are set to secondary by default, so they do not change bidding until someone makes them primary. Historical data from before the import is not included.

How to line the reports up before you compare

Do this once and write down the settings. Then every future comparison starts from the same place.

  • Pick one conversion that matters, such as a lead form, and compare that alone.
  • Use a full calendar month that ended at least a week ago, so late conversions have arrived.
  • In Google Ads, use the All conv. (by conv. time) column for that single action.
  • In GA4, use the conversions report in the Advertising section, filtered to Google Ads traffic.
  • Check that both tools use the same time zone, or note the difference.
  • Write down the counting method, conversion window and attribution settings on each side.

Which number to trust for which decision

Neither tool is the truth. Both are views of what happened. The useful question is which view fits the decision in front of you.

For bidding and judging Google Ads campaigns, use Google Ads conversions. That is what the bid strategy sees and works towards, so it is the number your changes affect. Make sure the right actions are primary.

For understanding the whole site, such as which channels bring visitors and where journeys stall, use GA4 key events. Google itself says the standard reports and explorations are not recommended for measuring media performance.

For money, use neither on its own. Check both against the business record: enquiries in the inbox, deals in the CRM, revenue in the accounts. If the gap between the tools and the business stays roughly steady, the trend is real. If that gap changes suddenly, look at measurement before you change the campaigns.

A steady, explained gap between GA4 and Google Ads is normal. A gap nobody can explain means decisions are being made on a number that does not mean what people think it means.
