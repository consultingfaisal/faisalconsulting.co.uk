Hiring a fractional CMO in the UK: the questions to ask before you sign

23 November 2026

The interview tells you whether you like the person and trust their judgement. The agreement decides whether the engagement actually works. Problems that surface a few months into a fractional CMO engagement are often easier to prevent at the signing stage, with a question nobody thought to ask.

This guide is about that second stage. You have a candidate you rate. You have a proposal in front of you. Before you sign, work through the questions below and make sure the answers are written down, not just agreed on a call.

Some of these are commercial. Some are legal duties that sit with you as the client, not with the person you hire. None of this replaces your own legal or tax advice, but it will help you know what to ask for.

What exactly is in scope, and what is not?

A fractional CMO owns the marketing function at board level, but for part of the week. That makes scope the first thing to pin down. Without it, the role drifts towards whatever is most urgent that week, which is rarely the most important work.

Ask for the scope in terms of outcomes and responsibilities, not activity. Owning the marketing plan and budget is a responsibility. Attending meetings is activity. The written scope should also say what is out of scope, such as hands-on campaign execution, design or day-to-day social media.

  • Which decisions can they make alone, and which need sign-off?
  • Do they hold budget authority, and up to what level?
  • Who in your business do they manage, if anyone?
  • What happens if you want to add work that sits outside the scope?

How many days, and when?

Fractional work usually means one to three days a week. The number matters less than how those days are used. Some businesses need a fixed day each week so the team knows when decisions get made. Others need flexible time around board meetings and launches.

Ask how availability works between those days. A quick answer to an urgent question on a Thursday is reasonable to expect. A full working session is not, unless it is agreed. Write down how unused days are handled, how extra days are agreed, and how much notice either side needs to move a day.

What will you see, and how often?

A fractional leader who works part of the week needs to report clearly, because you will not see them every day. Agree the reporting rhythm before you start. That usually means a short written update at a fixed interval and a fuller review against agreed measures each month or quarter.

Agree the measures too. If the engagement is meant to improve lead quality, say how lead quality will be measured and where the numbers will come from. If tracking is not yet good enough to trust, fixing it may need to be part of the scope before any performance target means much.

  • Who receives the reports: the founder, the board or both?
  • Which numbers will be reported, and from which system?
  • How will you both judge, at the first review, whether the engagement is working?

How long is the term, and how does it end?

Most fractional engagements need several months to show what they can do, because the early weeks go on diagnosis and foundations. A minimum term can make sense for that reason. So can a review point, where either side can change course without a dispute.

Look closely at notice. Check the notice period on both sides, whether it changes after a first period, and what happens to work in progress when the engagement ends. Ask for a written handover to be part of the agreement: current plans, live campaigns, open supplier contracts, and where every account and report sits.

Who decides the IR35 status?

Many fractional leaders work through their own limited company. When that happens, the off-payroll working rules may apply. HMRC guidance says the rules apply to all public sector clients and to medium and large private and voluntary sector clients. In those cases the client must decide the employment status of the worker.

If that is you, the guidance says you must take reasonable care, and you must give a Status Determination Statement with your conclusion and the reasons for it. That statement goes to the worker and to the person or organisation you contract with. You must also have a process for considering the worker's view if they disagree, and respond within 45 days. HMRC's check employment status for tax tool can help you reach a decision.

If your business is small under HMRC's size test, you do not make the determination, but you must confirm your size if asked. Either way, settle this before you sign, not after the first invoice. The site's IR35 explainer covers what tends to decide status in practice.

Will they handle your customers' personal data?

A marketing lead often needs access to your CRM, email platform, analytics and ad accounts. Some of those hold personal data about your customers. Ask what access they need, and whether they will process any of that data on your behalf.

The ICO's guidance is clear on one point. Every time a controller uses a processor to process personal data, there must be a written contract that binds the processor to the controller. If your fractional CMO, or their company, will act as a processor for you, that contract needs to be in place. Ask too whether they plan to use any other suppliers or tools to handle that data, because the guidance also requires a written contract between a processor and any sub-processor.

  • Give access through named user accounts in your own systems, not shared logins.
  • Keep ownership of every account with your business.
  • Agree how access is removed when the engagement ends.

Who owns the work they produce?

Strategy documents, brand guidelines, campaign plans, reporting templates and website copy all have value after the engagement ends. Do not assume you own them because you paid for them.

The Intellectual Property Office guidance on copyright says that a person working under a contract for services will usually keep the copyright in what they produce, unless there is a contractual agreement to the contrary. For commissioned work, it says the commissioner may only get a limited licence if copyright is not agreed in writing. If you want to own the work, the agreement should say so. If the CMO wants to reuse general frameworks or templates they brought with them, agree that too, so both sides know where the line is.

How will they work with your agency and team?

A fractional CMO often sits above an agency relationship, briefing it and holding it to account. That only works if everyone knows who holds the brief. Before you sign, decide whether the agency reports to the CMO, to you, or to both, and tell the agency.

The same applies inside the business. If your marketing manager will report to the fractional CMO, say so in writing and explain it to the team before day one. Unclear lines of authority are one of the quickest ways to waste the days you are paying for.

  • Can they review and renegotiate supplier contracts, or only recommend?
  • Who signs off agency invoices and briefs?
  • Who does the team go to when the CMO is not in?

None of these questions is difficult. Each one takes a few minutes to answer, and the answers belong in the agreement rather than in someone's memory of a call. A good candidate will welcome them, because clear terms protect them as much as you.
