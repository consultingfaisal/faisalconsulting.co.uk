Interim or fractional marketing director: which one do you need?

18 November 2026

Sooner or later, many businesses find they need a senior marketing leader they do not have. Someone has left. A plan has stalled. The board wants one person to own the number. Two kinds of outside help come up again and again: an interim marketing director and a fractional marketing director.

Both are senior. Both own marketing rather than just advising on it. The difference is how much of the week you get, for how long, and why you are bringing them in. Pick the wrong one and you either pay for time you cannot use, or you leave a gap that part time cover cannot fill.

This guide sets out the difference, the questions that decide it, and what to check before you agree terms. It does not cover fees, which depend on scope.

The short difference

An interim marketing director works full time for your business for a fixed period. The usual reason is a gap that needs covering now, such as a departure, a long leave, a restructure or a launch that cannot wait.

A fractional marketing director works for part of the week, on a regular pattern, over a longer run. The usual reason is that the business needs senior marketing judgement but does not yet have a full week of senior marketing work. The rest of their week goes to other clients.

So the two models answer different questions. Interim answers: who runs marketing while we sort this out? Fractional answers: who leads marketing when we cannot justify a full time director yet?

Start with the problem, not the model

Before you compare the two, write down in a few lines what is actually wrong or missing. Be specific. A vague brief makes both options look equally good.

Then add two facts that shape everything else: when the gap starts, and when you expect it to end. A gap with a clear end date points one way. A need with no end in sight points another.

  • What has stopped, or is at risk of stopping, if nobody senior owns it?
  • Is this a temporary gap, or a permanent need you have not filled yet?
  • Who will the person report to, and who will report to them?
  • What result will you judge them on at the end?

The questions that point one way or the other

Most decisions come down to a handful of questions. Answer them honestly and the right model is usually clear.

  • How much senior work is there each week? If a team, several agencies and a live budget need daily decisions, part of the week may not be enough. If the work is planning, direction and oversight, it often is.
  • How fast do you need someone? A departure that leaves campaigns running with nobody in charge needs cover quickly. A plan you want to improve over the next year can wait for the right fit.
  • How long is the need? A fixed gap, such as cover until a permanent hire starts, suits a fixed term. An open need suits an arrangement you review as you go.
  • Who manages the team day to day? Someone on a share of the week can lead a team, but the team needs to be able to work between their days.
  • Can the business act on decisions quickly? Full time cover keeps pace with a busy function. Part time leadership still needs people in the business to act on what is decided.

When interim cover tends to fit

Interim is built for situations where the seat is empty and the work behind it is still full time. The aim is continuity first, then direction.

Interim is also useful when a business needs one person to be visibly in charge, for example during a restructure, where staff and agencies need a single senior contact every day.

  • A marketing director or head of marketing has left and the role is being recruited permanently.
  • A senior leader is on long leave, such as parental leave, and the role needs covering in full.
  • A merger, acquisition or restructure has left nobody clearly owning marketing.
  • A launch or campaign is too close and too important to run without full time senior ownership.

When fractional tends to fit

Fractional is built for businesses that need a senior owner for marketing, but not five days a week of it. The aim is steady leadership over months rather than cover for a gap.

It also suits a business that is not yet sure what the long term marketing role should look like. A few months of fractional leadership can make that clearer before you commit to a permanent salary.

  • A growing business with a small team and nobody senior leading it.
  • A business that relies on agencies but has no one in house to brief them and hold them to a result.
  • An owner who is still making the marketing decisions alongside everything else.
  • A business that needs its tracking, budget and reporting sorted out, then kept on track.

When neither is the answer

Sometimes the honest answer is that you do not need a senior leader at all, or you need a permanent one.

If the plan is clear and what you lack is people to run campaigns, more delivery help may serve you better than more leadership. An agency or a specialist can do that.

If you need a review, a second opinion or coaching rather than someone to own the result, a consultant or adviser is a lighter option.

If there is enough senior marketing work to fill a full week, and the need is permanent, a full time hire is often the better long term choice. Interim or fractional cover can still help while you recruit.

Moving between the two

The two models are not a one time choice. Needs change, and the arrangement can change with them.

A common pattern is interim cover to steady things after a departure, followed by a fractional arrangement once the function is stable and the full time load has eased. The reverse also happens. A fractional director may step up to more days for a period, such as a launch, and then step back.

Whichever you start with, agree at the outset how the arrangement will be reviewed and how it will end. That might be a handover to a permanent hire, a reduction in days, or a clean finish once the job is done. A clear end point protects both sides.

Check employment status before you sign

If the person works through their own limited company, the UK off-payroll working rules, often called IR35, may apply. HMRC guidance says these rules make sure a worker who would have been an employee if engaged directly pays broadly the same Income Tax and National Insurance as an employee would.

In most cases the client is responsible for deciding the worker's status. If you are a small client outside the public sector, the worker's own company makes that decision instead. HMRC sets out how to tell whether your business counts as small.

Where the client decides, HMRC expects you to take reasonable care, to give your conclusion and the reasons for it in a status determination statement, and to keep records. HMRC's check employment status for tax tool can help you make the determination.

Status depends on how the work is actually done, not on the job title. A full time engagement for one business looks different from part time work spread across several clients, so check each engagement on its own facts. This is general information, not tax advice.

The choice between interim and fractional comes down to time, duration and purpose. Full time cover for a fixed period fits a gap that cannot wait. Part of the week over a longer run fits a business that needs senior leadership but not a full week of it. Work out what is wrong, how long the need will last and how much senior work there really is, and the answer is usually clear.
