Fix underperforming Google Ads: where to start when results drop

21 November 2026

An account that was working last month and is not working now needs a different approach from one that never worked. Something changed. Your job is to find what changed before you change anything else.

The usual reaction is to raise bids, rewrite ads or move budget. Each of those adds a new change on top of the one you have not found yet. Then you have two unknowns instead of one.

Google says plainly that it is normal for campaign performance to vary. So the first question is whether this is a real drop or normal noise. The steps below run in a set order. Each one rules something out before you move to the next.

Make sure the drop is real

Start with the numbers themselves. Clicks, impressions and cost refresh roughly every hour. Conversions are slower, and how slow depends on your attribution model and conversion type. Impression share and auction insights are typically updated once a day.

Conversions also arrive late for a second reason. People do not always act on the day they click. Google notes that conversions can be reported up to 90 days after the click, depending on the conversion window. Spend for recent days is already complete, so those days can show fewer conversions and a higher cost per conversion than they will end up with.

To see how long your customers usually take, set a date range that ends at least 30 days ago. Then segment by Conversions and choose Days to conversion. If most conversions land within a day or two, a drop over the last week is more likely to be real. If they take a fortnight, the last week will almost always look weak.

  • Compare equal periods, such as the last 14 days against the 14 days before.
  • Leave today out of the range. It is never complete.
  • Judge the metric your bid strategy works towards. On a target cost per conversion strategy, cost per conversion tells you more than clicks.

Read the change history before you guess

If the drop is real, look for the change that caused it. Change history sits in the Campaigns menu. It lists changes to the account, campaigns and ad groups over the past two years, and lines them up against impressions, clicks, conversions and cost.

Filter by change type, such as budget or keyword edits, and set the date range to cover the days before the drop. Look for edits made by people, by tools connected through the API, and by the Google Ads system. A paused ad group, a new negative keyword list or a changed conversion goal can all explain a sudden fall.

Change history does not show everything. Google notes that some account level settings are not listed. So an empty history is a clue, not a final answer.

Use explanations to narrow it down

Google Ads has a built in feature called explanations. It compares two date ranges and points to the likely causes of a significant change. It covers Search, Performance Max, Demand Gen, Display, Video and App campaigns.

Hover over a blue dotted value in the campaigns or ad groups table, or over the performance graph, and select View explanations. The causes it looks for include bid and budget changes, conversion settings, conversion delay, eligibility such as paused items or disapprovals, targeting changes and auction competition.

Two limits matter. Explanations will not show if the date range includes today or goes back further than the last 90 days. The two periods also need to be the same length and sit next to each other.

Check conversion tracking is still recording

A drop in conversions is often a drop in measurement, not in customers. A website update, a new form or a removed tag can stop conversions recording while leads keep coming in.

Open the Goals menu and then Summary. The status column shows each conversion action. Active means it is working as intended. Needs attention means there are problems to fix but it has not stopped recording. Misconfigured means conversions have stopped recording because of a setup error or a broken tag. Awaiting conversions means none have been recorded in the last seven days.

Then compare with the business. If the sales team or the inbox received the usual number of enquiries, and the account shows fewer, the fault is in tracking. Send a test enquiry and confirm it appears.

  • If tracking broke for a set period and you use Smart Bidding, look at data exclusions. They tell bidding to ignore the conversion data from the affected dates.
  • Exclusions apply to clicks, so include the days before the outage that your conversion delay covers.
  • Google advises applying them quickly, not removing them once applied, and not using them often or for long periods. Bids and spend can still dip for a short time afterwards.

Look at the bid strategy status

If you use automated bidding, open Bid strategies in the Tools menu and read the status column. Hover over the status for the reason.

Learning is a reminder that the setup changed recently. Google says it never lasts more than seven days. It is triggered by a new or reactivated strategy, by adding or removing campaigns, ad groups or keywords, or by a significant change to the conversion goals. Google also says that changes to budgets, targets, ads or targeting do not trigger learning.

Limited means something is holding the strategy back. The hover text says whether it is the budget, bid limits, low search volume or the strategy itself. Misconfigured often points back to conversion settings, such as an action that is not included in Conversions.

Rule out eligibility, policy and billing

Ads that cannot run will not perform. Google's own troubleshooting list includes policy and ad review status, payment and billing issues, and campaigns that have reached their scheduled end date.

Check for disapproved or limited ads and keywords, and read the reason given. Check that payment has not failed. Check the end dates on campaigns that were set up for a season or a promotion. If you want to see whether an ad is showing, Google points to the Ad preview and diagnosis tool rather than searching for it yourself.

  • Disapproved ads, assets or keywords in the campaigns that dropped.
  • Payment problems or a billing change around the date of the drop.
  • End dates, ad schedules and budgets that have run out.

Then look at the auction

Only when the account itself checks out should you look outside it. Competitors change their bids and budgets, and that shows up in your results without any change on your side.

Add the impression share columns. Search lost IS (budget) means the money ran out. Search lost IS (rank) means your ad rank lost to others. Auction insights shows which other advertisers appear alongside you and how often. A new name near the top, or an existing one appearing more often, is worth noting.

Seasonal demand matters too. A fall in impressions with steady impression share usually means fewer people searched, not that you lost ground.

Change one thing at a time

Once you know the cause, fix that and nothing else. Give it time to show in the data, allowing for your conversion delay. Write down what you changed and when, so the next person reading change history knows why.

If you change bids, budgets and ads in one afternoon, you will never know which change helped. You will also be back where you started next time results drop.

Most sudden drops trace back to one of a few causes: incomplete data, a change someone made, broken tracking, a bidding reset, ads that cannot run, or a shift in the auction. Checking them in order saves money and guesswork.

If you work through these steps and still cannot explain the drop, or the tracking and the business numbers disagree, a marketing performance audit is the next step. It traces spend and measurement back to the source, then sets out the few fixes that will move the commercial number.
