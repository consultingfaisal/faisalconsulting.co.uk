Is your Google Ads account wasting money? How to read the search terms report

12 November 2026

Many Google Ads accounts have the same blind spot. The team knows which keywords they bid on. Far fewer know what people actually typed before they clicked.

That gap is where money leaks. A keyword is a target. The search term is what really happened. Google shows you the difference in one place: the search terms report.

This guide covers where to find the report, how to read it line by line, what to do with what you find, and what the report will not tell you.

Keywords and search terms are not the same thing

Google defines a search term as the word or set of words a person enters when they search. A keyword is what you add to an ad group to reach those people. One keyword can trigger your ad on many different searches.

How many depends on match type. A broad match keyword can show your ad on searches that are only related to it. The report has a match type column that shows how closely each search term relates to your keyword. Close variants are labelled, for example Exact match (close variant).

The search term's match type can differ from the keyword's. A broad match keyword can produce a search term shown as exact match. So do not assume your exact match keywords are the only thing spending money.

Where to find the report

In Google Ads, open Campaigns. In the section menu, open Insights and reports, then Search terms.

Two settings make the report far more useful. Both take less than a minute.

  • Turn on the Keyword column. It is hidden by default. Select the columns icon, choose Attributes, tick Keyword and select Apply. Now you can see which of your keywords matched each search.
  • Pick a date range long enough to show patterns. A single week often has too few clicks per term to judge anything.
  • Use Segment if you need it. You can split the report by time, conversions, device or network.

Sort by cost, then read every line

Sort the report by cost, highest first. The terms at the top are where most of your money went, so they deserve the most attention.

Then read each line and ask one question: would I happily pay for this click again? Most terms fall into one of four groups.

Take a plumber in Leeds as an example. A search for emergency plumber leeds is exactly right. A search for plumber apprenticeship leeds is a job seeker. A search for how to fix a dripping tap is someone who wants to do it themselves. None of the last two will become a customer, however good the ad is.

  • Right: the search matches what you sell, where you sell it.
  • Wrong intent: jobs, training, free, DIY, or a product you do not offer.
  • Wrong place: a town or country you do not serve.
  • Unclear: research searches that might buy later. Watch these before you block them.

Check conversions, not just clicks

A term that costs money is not automatically waste. Add the conversions and cost per conversion columns, and look at what each term actually produced.

Before you trust those numbers, make sure your conversion tracking works. If conversions are missing or counted twice, every decision you make from this report will be wrong. Checking tracking first is the most common step people skip.

Be careful with small numbers. A term with two clicks and no sale has not proved anything yet. A term that has spent steadily for weeks with nothing to show for it has.

If you run Performance Max, it has its own search terms report. Google advises giving conversion data priority over clicks and impressions there, because Performance Max can drive conversions without a direct click.

Turn waste into negative keywords

A negative keyword stops your ad showing on searches that contain it. You can add them straight from the report. Tick the search terms you want to exclude, select Add as negative keyword, then choose where the negative applies: the ad group, the campaign, or a negative keyword list.

Negatives added this way use exact match by default. That blocks the exact search and nothing else, which is often too narrow. Think about whether a single word, such as jobs or free, is the real problem, and add that instead.

Negative keywords behave differently from normal keywords. Google is clear on these points.

  • Negatives do not match close variants. Add plurals and synonyms yourself if you want them blocked.
  • Broad match negative: the ad will not show if the search contains all of your negative words, in any order.
  • Phrase match negative: the ad will not show if the search contains the words in the same order, even with extra words.
  • Exact match negative: the ad will not show only when the search is exactly those words.
  • Account level negatives sit under Admin, then Account settings, then Negative keywords. They apply to search and shopping inventory across Search, Performance Max, App, Shopping, Smart and Local campaigns, up to 1,000 terms.

What the report will not show you

The report is not a full list of every search. Google leaves out search terms with too little activity, to protect privacy. Expect the report to show your biggest leaks, not every small one.

Store visits and store sales conversions are not available in the report. Performance Max search terms data only goes back to March 2023.

For Performance Max on Display and Video, negative keywords do not apply. Google points you to excluded content keywords in the Content Suitability settings instead.

Do not over-block

It is tempting to block anything that looks odd. Google itself warns that too many negative keywords can reduce how many customers your ads reach.

The risk is highest with broad match negatives on common words. Blocking the word cheap might stop wasted clicks, but it might also block buyers who search for the best price. Check whether a word also appears in searches that convert before you block it.

The report also works the other way. When a search term converts well and is not yet a keyword, consider adding it as one, so you can control its bid and ad copy.

A simple weekly routine

You do not need to live in this report. A short, regular check catches most waste before it adds up.

  • Open the search terms report and sort by cost.
  • Read the top terms and mark each one right, wrong intent, wrong place or unclear.
  • Add clear waste as negatives at the right level, with the right match type.
  • Add strong converting terms as keywords.
  • Note anything you cannot explain, and check your tracking before you act on it.

If the report shows spend you cannot explain, or conversions you do not trust, the problem is usually bigger than one campaign. That is what a marketing performance audit is for: tracing wasted spend and broken tracking back to the source, then fixing the few issues that move the commercial number.
