Fractional marketing director, agency or in-house hire: which fits a UK SME

17 November 2026

When a growing business needs more from its marketing, three options usually come up. Bring in a fractional marketing director. Hire an agency. Or employ someone in house.

Each one can work well. Each one can also be the wrong answer for the same business at a different stage. The choice gets easier when you stop comparing job titles and start with what the business actually needs done.

This guide sets out three tests you can apply yourself: how much work there is and what kind, which skills you already have, and who should control the budget. It does not cover fees. Those depend on scope.

Three options, three different jobs

It helps to be clear about what each option is for before you compare them.

These are not ranked. A business can sensibly use one, two or all three at once. The question is which gap you are filling right now.

  • A fractional marketing director leads. They own the plan, the budget decisions and the results, for part of the week, on a regular basis.
  • An agency delivers. It carries out the work it is briefed to do, such as paid search, social, design or content, often with specialist skills a small team would not have.
  • An in-house hire is part of the business. They work only for you, every working day, and build knowledge of your customers and products over time. The role can be senior or hands on, depending on who you hire.

Start with the work, not the title

Write down the marketing work the business needs over the next six to twelve months. Then sort it into two piles.

The first pile is senior work. Setting priorities, deciding where the budget goes, choosing which channels to stop, briefing others and reporting results to the owner or board. The second pile is delivery work. Building campaigns, writing, designing, posting, running ad accounts and producing reports.

Now look at the size of each pile.

If there is a lot of delivery work and the plan is already clear, the gap is capacity. An agency or a hands-on hire is built for that. Adding a senior leader will not get the campaigns out any faster.

If the delivery is happening but nobody is setting the direction, the gap is leadership. Ask whether there is a full week of senior work. If there is, a full-time senior hire may make sense. If there is a day or two a week of it, that is the situation a fractional role is designed for.

If both piles are large, you may need both kinds of help. That is common, and it is a reason to decide who leads before you decide who delivers.

Look at the skills you already have

The second test is what is already inside the business. Make a simple list of who does marketing today and what they are good at.

Many SMEs have one or two capable marketers who are stretched across everything. Others have an owner who makes the marketing decisions alongside running the company. Some have no one at all.

Your answers point in different directions.

  • Strong doers, no senior lead: the team can deliver, but nobody is setting priorities or checking results. Leadership is the missing piece.
  • A senior lead, no specialist skills: someone can set direction, but the business lacks skills such as paid media or design. An agency or freelancer can fill that specific gap.
  • Nobody in house: you need both direction and delivery. Decide which you need first, because the person leading will shape what the delivery should look like.
  • A team that is simply too small: the plan is sound and the skills are there, but there are not enough hours. An in-house hire or extra agency support adds capacity.

Decide who controls the budget

The third test is often skipped. Ask who should decide where the marketing money goes, and who should answer for what it produces.

An agency is usually paid to run a part of your marketing. It can advise on budget, and good agencies do. But it is rarely placed to judge its own channel against others, or to recommend that spend moves away from the work it delivers. That is not a criticism. It is how the arrangement is set up.

So if your main worry is that money is spread across channels and nobody can say which ones work, the fix is a person on your side of the table. That can be an in-house leader or a fractional one. What matters is that they are accountable to you for the commercial result, not for activity in one channel.

If the budget is small and mostly in one channel, the case for separate budget ownership is weaker. A capable agency with clear targets and honest reporting may be all you need, as long as someone in the business checks the numbers.

What employing someone involves

An in-house hire is the only option of the three where you become the employer. That brings duties beyond salary, and it is worth listing them before you compare costs.

The government's step by step guide to employing someone for the first time covers, among other things, the points below. If you already employ staff, most of this is in place. If you do not, it is real work to set up.

A fractional director or an agency usually invoices you as a supplier instead. If a fractional director works through their own limited company, check the off-payroll working rules, often called IR35. There is a page on this site that explains how they apply to fractional marketing roles.

  • Checking that the person counts as an employee, and what the National Minimum Wage is.
  • Paying National Insurance for your employees, and allowing for sick pay and maternity or paternity pay.
  • Registering with HMRC as an employer and setting up PAYE, so you can pay tax and National Insurance for your employees.
  • Understanding your workplace pension responsibilities, including what you pay towards an employee's pension.
  • Getting Employers' Liability insurance.

Questions that point to an answer

If you are still unsure, answer these honestly. They will not decide for you, but they usually make the right option obvious.

  • Is there a full week of senior marketing work, or a day or two?
  • Is the plan clear, so the real gap is getting the work done?
  • Does anyone in the business own the marketing result today?
  • Which specialist skills are missing, and how often would you use them?
  • Who should decide when to cut a channel, and are they independent of it?
  • How quickly do you need help to start, and for how long?
  • Can the business take on the duties of employing someone, if it does not already?

Using more than one option

These options are not exclusive. Many SMEs end up with a mix, and the mix changes as the business grows.

A common pattern is a senior lead, either fractional or in house, who sets the plan and holds an agency or a small team to it. Another is an in-house marketer who delivers most of the work, with an agency for one specialist channel. The important thing is that everyone knows who leads and who delivers.

Whatever you choose, agree three things at the start. What marketing is meant to achieve for the business. How results will be measured. And when you will review whether the arrangement still fits. A choice that was right at one stage can be wrong a year later.
