Fractional CMO or fractional marketing director: what is the difference in the UK

13 November 2026

If you search for senior marketing help on a part time basis, you will meet two titles again and again. One is fractional CMO. The other is fractional marketing director. Some people use both on the same profile.

It is fair to ask whether you are looking at two different jobs. In most cases you are not. Both describe a senior person who owns marketing and answers for its results, but works for you on a share of the week rather than full time.

This guide explains what each title signals, why they overlap so much, and what to check instead of the label. It is written for UK business owners and boards who are deciding what to look for, not for people choosing a job title for themselves.

Two titles, one level of role

CMO stands for chief marketing officer. Marketing director is the other common name for the same seat. Both point at the most senior marketing role in the company: the person who sets the plan, owns the budget and is accountable for what marketing delivers.

The word fractional means the same thing in both cases. You get that senior person for part of their time, often a set number of days a week or month, instead of employing them full time. Part time CMO and part time marketing director are other ways of saying the same thing.

So the honest answer to the question in the title is that the difference is mostly about naming, not seniority. The work behind the title is what you need to pin down.

Why a business picks one title over the other

The choice usually follows the shape of your own leadership team. If the people around the table are called chief executive, chief financial officer and chief operating officer, a chief marketing officer fits that pattern. If your senior people are directors of finance, operations and sales, marketing director fits better.

Investors and boards can also shape the choice. Some expect to see a chief title against each function. Others care only that a named person owns the number.

Neither choice changes what the role can do. A title that matches your structure simply makes reporting lines easier for your team, your agencies and your board to understand.

Fractional, interim and consultant: the words around the title

The word in front of the title often tells you more than the title itself. It is worth being clear on three of them before you start talking to people.

The line between them is not about the name on the contract. It is about whether the person owns the outcome, and how much of their time you get.

  • Fractional or part time: a senior leader who owns marketing on a regular share of the week, usually over several months, alongside your team.
  • Interim: a senior leader who works full time for a fixed period, often to cover a gap such as a departure or a long leave.
  • Consultant or adviser: someone who reviews, recommends or coaches, but does not usually own the plan, the budget or the team day to day.

The questions that matter more than the title

Two people with the same title can offer very different jobs. Ask the same questions of everyone you speak to, whatever they call themselves. The answers tell you what you are actually buying.

  • What commercial result will you be accountable for, and how will we both know if it is working?
  • Who will you report to, and will you attend board or leadership meetings?
  • Will you own the marketing budget, or advise the person who does?
  • Will you manage our team and agencies directly, or work through someone else?
  • How many days will you give us, and how is that time split between planning and doing?
  • What will we get from you in the first month?
  • How will you report on progress, and to whom?
  • What happens at the end: a handover, a hire, or an ongoing role?

Where the difference does show up

Although the titles overlap, the role you write can lean one way or the other. It helps to know which way you need it to lean.

Some roles lean towards the board. The person spends more of their time on strategy, budget, investor questions and the link between marketing and the rest of the business. That often sits more naturally under a chief title.

Other roles lean towards running the function. The person spends more time leading the team, briefing agencies, fixing tracking and getting campaigns right week to week. That often sits more naturally under a director title.

Most fractional roles need some of both. What matters is that you decide the balance before you start, write it down, and agree how the days will be used.

Signs the title does not match the job

A mismatch between title and remit causes trouble on both sides. Watch for these before you agree terms.

  • The role has a chief or director title but no say over the budget.
  • Nobody can tell you who the person reports to, or what they report on.
  • The brief is mostly about producing content or running one channel, which is usually a specialist role rather than a leadership one.
  • The days on offer are too few for the scope. A long list of duties on very little time means some of them will not get done.
  • Success is described as activity, such as posts published or campaigns launched, rather than a commercial result.

How to write the role so you get the right person

Start with the problem, not the title. Write down what is not working, what the business needs marketing to deliver over the next six to twelve months, and what the person will own.

Then pick the title that fits your structure, and say plainly in the brief that you would consider either title. That widens the field. Many experienced people describe themselves both ways, and some only search for one of the two terms.

Finally, agree in writing how the time will be used, who the person reports to, what decisions they can take without sign off, and how you will review progress. Those points will shape the engagement far more than the word on the email signature.
