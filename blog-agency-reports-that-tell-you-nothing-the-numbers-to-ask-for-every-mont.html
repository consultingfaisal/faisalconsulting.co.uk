Agency reports that tell you nothing: the numbers to ask for every month

26 November 2026

Most agency reports arrive on time and look professional. Many of them still leave you unable to answer a simple question: did marketing help the business last month?

The usual problem is not missing data. It is the wrong data, or the right data without context. Pages of clicks, impressions and charts that go up and to the right can hide the fact that nothing useful happened.

This guide sets out the numbers worth asking for every month, what each one tells you, and the checks to make before you rely on them. It is about the content of the report. How to run the wider relationship with your agency is covered in a separate guide on holding an agency to account.

Why so many reports say so little

A report that tells you nothing usually has one of three faults. It lists activity instead of results, such as posts published or campaigns launched. It leads with numbers that are easy to grow, such as impressions and clicks. Or it shows this month on its own, with nothing to compare it against.

None of these is dishonest in itself. Activity matters and clicks are a real cost. But they are inputs. You pay an agency for what those inputs produce, so the report should start there.

A useful test: could you read the first page and explain to a colleague, in two sentences, whether things got better or worse and why? If not, the report needs changing.

Start with the result the business cares about

The first number in the report should be the outcome the work is meant to support. For most businesses that is one of a few things: qualified enquiries, booked appointments, online sales or revenue.

Agree this number before the report is built, and agree where it comes from. If it lives in your CRM or booking system rather than in Google Ads, the agency may need you to send it each month. That is normal. A report that only uses the agency's own platforms can never show whether the leads turned into customers.

Conversions, and which ones are being counted

Conversion figures are where most confusion starts, because the same word can mean different things in different places.

In Google Ads, the Conversions column only counts primary conversion actions. Secondary actions are for observation. They appear in the All conversions column and are not used for bidding. Google Ads also counts view-through conversions in All conversions, not in Conversions. So a report that quotes All conversions can look much better than one that quotes Conversions for exactly the same month.

In Google Analytics 4, important actions are called key events. A conversion now means an action you use to measure ad campaigns and to guide bidding. Google's flow is event, then key event, then conversion. When an agency quotes a figure from GA4, ask whether it is a key event count or a conversion that is also used in Google Ads.

Ask the agency to list, once, every action it counts as a conversion and whether each is primary or secondary. Then make sure the report names the column it uses. If a phone click, a newsletter sign up and a completed enquiry are all counted the same, the total will not mean much.

Cost per result and the value behind it

Once you trust the conversion count, cost per result is the next number to watch. Google Ads works out cost per conversion by dividing total cost by the number in the Conversions column. If the conversion count includes the wrong actions, the cost per conversion will look better than it is.

If you sell online, or can put a value on a lead, ask for conversion value too. In Google Ads the conversion value column adds up the values of the conversions in the Conversions column. Value lets you judge spend against money coming in, not just against a count of actions.

Ask for both figures by campaign or channel, not only as one total. A healthy average can hide one campaign doing well and another losing money.

What you are missing: impression share

Results tell you what happened. Impression share helps you see what did not happen. Google defines Search impression share as the impressions you received on the Search Network divided by the estimated number you were eligible to receive.

Two related figures explain the gap. Search lost impression share due to budget is the share of time your ads did not show because the budget ran out. Search lost impression share due to rank is the share of time they did not show because of poor Ad Rank. Google reports lost share due to budget at campaign level only.

These two numbers point to different fixes. Share lost to budget raises the question of whether to spend more on a campaign that works. Share lost to rank points to bids, ad quality or landing pages. A report that includes them gives you a far better basis for budget conversations than clicks alone.

Compare like with like

A number on its own says very little. Ask for each headline figure next to the previous month and the same month last year. The year on year view matters for any business with seasonal demand, where a dip against last month may be entirely normal.

Two details in Google Ads affect comparisons. First, the standard conversion columns are reported against the date of the ad click, not the date of the conversion. A click in late October that converts in early November is counted in October. Google offers separate columns by conversion time if you want to see conversions by the day they happened. Second, Google notes that conversion reporting can lag by 24 to 48 hours. A report pulled on the first of the month may undercount the last few days, and those figures can rise later.

Neither is a problem if everyone knows about it. Ask the agency to state which date basis it uses and to pull the report after the lag has passed.

Lead quality, from your side of the table

The agency can see how many enquiries arrived. Often only you can see what happened next. Were they the right kind of customer? Did they book, buy or go quiet?

Keep a simple monthly count from your own records: enquiries received, enquiries that were a good fit, and enquiries that became customers. Share it with the agency. It turns the report from a one way update into a shared view of what is working, and it gives the agency a fair chance to steer spend towards the leads you actually want.

If you can, feed those outcomes back into Google Ads as offline conversions. Then bidding can learn from sales rather than from form fills alone.

The page you should get every month

You do not need a long report. One clear page covers most of what a business owner needs. The detail can sit behind it for anyone who wants to look.

If a figure has moved a long way, the page should say why the agency thinks it moved and what it plans to do. A short sentence is enough.

  • The business result you agreed, against last month and the same month last year.
  • Spend by channel or campaign.
  • Conversions by campaign, with the column named, and cost per conversion.
  • Conversion value, if you sell online or have put a value on leads.
  • Search impression share, with share lost to budget and share lost to rank.
  • Lead quality from your own records: good fit and converted.
  • What changed in the account, what the agency learned, and what it will do next month.
  • Any tracking problems found, and when they were fixed.

A good monthly report is short, starts with the result, names its sources and compares like with like. If your agency cannot produce one, the reason is often that tracking or conversion settings need fixing first.

That is what a marketing performance audit looks at: whether the numbers in your reports can be trusted, and which few changes would make them useful for decisions.
