Running Google Ads on a small budget: what to cut first

28 November 2026

A small Google Ads budget is not a problem in itself. Plenty of local and specialist businesses run on modest daily spend. The problem is a small budget spread thinly across places, people and hours that were never going to buy.

Most accounts are not built that way on purpose. Several default settings reach wider than a small advertiser needs. Each one is reasonable for a large account. Together they can soak up a small budget before it reaches the searches that matter.

This guide is about ad spend only. It sets out what to cut first, in an order that protects the clicks most likely to become enquiries. Every setting named here is taken from Google's own help pages.

Know what your budget actually allows

Start by reading the budget the way Google reads it. The number you set is an average daily budget, not a hard daily cap.

Google says that on a given day a campaign might spend up to twice its average daily budget, to take advantage of changes in traffic. Over the month it balances out. Google states you will spend no more than 30.4 times your average daily budget in a month.

So a quiet Monday and an expensive Tuesday are normal. Judge spend over a week or a month, not a single day. If a monthly figure is what matters to you, divide it by 30.4 to find the daily budget to set.

Cut the networks you did not choose

A Search campaign can show your ads in more places than Google Search. Check this before anything else, because it changes where every pound goes.

Google search partners are included by default when you create a Search campaign. Partner placements include other sites' search results, site directory pages and YouTube search results. Google also says it does not report which website your ad appeared on in the Search Network, except for Performance Max.

A Search campaign can also include the Google Display Network. That puts your ads on websites and apps, using your Search campaign settings and the same overall budget.

For a small budget, the simplest test is to start with Google Search only. You can always add partners back once you know what Search alone produces.

  • Open Campaigns and select the campaign settings.
  • Open Networks.
  • To show ads only on Google Search, leave only the Google Search Network box ticked. Untick Include Google search partners and the Display Network if they are on.
  • Save, and note the date so you can compare before and after.

Cut people outside the area you serve

Location targeting has two options, and the default is the wider one.

Presence or interest is Google's default and recommended option. It reaches people in, regularly in, or who have shown interest in your target locations. Presence reaches only people in or regularly in your target locations.

Interest suits some businesses. A hotel or a holiday let wants people planning a trip from somewhere else. A plumber, a clinic or a local shop usually does not. Someone researching your town from another country is unlikely to book a visit next week.

If you only serve people where you are, switch the campaign to Presence. Then look at the locations report after a few weeks to see where clicks come from.

Cut the hours nobody can answer

If your leads come by phone or need a quick reply, a click at three in the morning may be worth less than one at ten. An ad schedule lets you choose when ads can show.

Google says that once you set an ad schedule, your ads are only eligible to show during the times you specify. You can create up to six schedules a day for each campaign. You set them from the Ad schedule page in the Campaigns menu, using the pencil icon.

Two points to watch. Schedules use your account's time zone, so adjust if your customers are somewhere else. And do not cut hours on a hunch. Look at when enquiries actually arrive, then trim the hours that cost money and produce nothing.

Cut campaigns before you thin out every one

A common reflex is to lower every campaign's budget a little. On a small account that often leaves several campaigns each too small to show much at all.

It usually works better to fund fewer campaigns properly. Keep the one or two that cover what you most want to sell. Pause the rest until there is room for them.

Google gives you two signals to judge this. The first is the Limited by budget status, which Google uses when a campaign is underperforming because of its budget, or when Maximize clicks could get more traffic with more budget.

The second is the Search lost IS (budget) column. Google defines it as the percentage of time your ads were not shown on the Search Network because of insufficient budget. It is available at campaign level. Add it from the columns icon, under Competitive metrics.

Compare it with Search lost IS (rank), which counts the times your ads did not show because of poor Ad Rank. If most of the loss is rank, more budget is not the first fix.

Cut the searches that never pay

Once the settings are tight, look at what people actually typed. The search terms report shows the real searches behind your clicks. On a small budget, a handful of wrong searches can take a large share of the money.

Sort by cost and ask of each line whether you would pay for that click again. Add clear waste as negative keywords. Our guide to reading the search terms report covers this step in detail, including how not to over-block.

Put a ceiling on what a click can cost

Many small accounts use Maximize clicks. Google describes it as a strategy that sets your bids to get as many clicks as possible within your budget.

It has an optional maximum CPC bid limit. Google says this can help control costs if your CPCs are higher than you want. Google also warns that if the limit is too low, the strategy may not be able to reach your goal. Bid adjustments apply on top of the limit.

A ceiling is useful when a few expensive clicks eat the day's budget. Set it from what a click is worth to you, not from a guess at what competitors pay.

What not to cut

Some things look like savings and cost you more. The first is conversion tracking. Without it you cannot tell which searches, hours or places bring enquiries, so every cut above becomes a guess. Check it records real leads before you change anything else.

The second is time to judge. A small budget produces small numbers. Change one thing, give it long enough to collect clicks, then compare. Changing five settings in one week tells you nothing about which one helped.

A small budget rewards a tidy account. Narrow the networks, the places and the hours first. Then fund fewer campaigns properly and clear out the searches that never pay.
