Planning next year's marketing budget when last year's numbers do not add up

19 November 2026

Budget season usually starts with a spreadsheet and a problem. Finance has one number for marketing spend. The agency report has another. Google Ads says one thing about leads, the CRM says something else, and nobody is sure which year to compare against.

It is tempting to take last year's total, add a percentage and move on. That repeats last year's mistakes at a slightly higher cost.

This guide covers how to work out which numbers you can trust, how to plan around the ones you cannot, and how to write a budget that still makes sense when someone asks hard questions about it.

Why last year's numbers rarely agree

Different systems answer different questions. Your accounts record money when it is invoiced or paid. Ad platforms record cost when the ad runs. Analytics tools record what happened on the website. The CRM records what the sales team logged.

None of these is wrong on its own terms. The trouble starts when they are put side by side as if they measure the same thing. Before you plan anything, accept that you are reconciling sources, not hunting for the one true figure.

Start with the money that actually left the business

The most reliable number you have is usually what you paid. Ask finance for every marketing cost from the last twelve months, by supplier and by month. Include media, agency fees, freelancers, software, events and print.

Then sort it into three groups. Working media is money that bought ads or placements. Fees are what you paid people to run things. Tools are subscriptions and software. Seeing the three side by side shows how much of the budget actually reached customers.

  • List each supplier once, with its yearly total.
  • Flag any cost that nobody can explain or that has no named owner.
  • Note contracts that renew automatically, and when.

Reconcile ad spend before you judge results

Next, compare what each ad platform says you spent with what finance says you paid. Expect some difference, because billing dates do not always line up with calendar months.

It also helps to know how platforms pace spend. Google Ads explains that a campaign can spend up to two times its average daily budget on a given day, for most campaigns. Over a month, it is limited to 30.4 times the average daily budget, which is the average number of days in a month. So a daily budget is not a daily cap, and a busy week can look like overspend when it is not.

If the gap between the platform and the invoices is large and you cannot explain it, stop there and find out why. A budget built on spend figures you do not trust will not hold up.

Check which conversions you are counting

Results are harder than spend. Start by asking what each report calls a conversion, and when it counts it.

In Google Ads, the standard conversion columns are calculated by the time of the click, not the time of the conversion. There is a separate column, Conversions (by conv. time), that reports by the date the conversion happened. Google also notes that conversions can be reported up to 90 days after the click, and that data can take 24 to 48 hours to process. So the most recent weeks of any year will look weaker than they really were.

Google is clear that Google Ads and other reporting tools will not match exactly. Its help pages point to attribution models, the date a conversion is credited to, conversion settings and invalid traffic that is removed after the fact. A gap between Google Ads and Google Analytics is expected. A gap you cannot explain is the one to chase.

The most useful check is the simplest. Pick one month and trace a handful of real enquiries from the CRM back to their source. If the ad platform claims far more leads than sales ever saw, the problem is in what is being counted, not in the campaign.

Know how much history you still have

Year on year comparisons depend on data you may no longer hold. In Google Analytics, a standard property can keep user level and event data for 2 months or 14 months. Google states that this setting does not affect standard aggregated reports. It only affects explorations and funnel reports.

In practice, your standard reports may still show last year's totals, while a detailed exploration of the same period comes back empty. Check the retention setting now, before you need the data. If it is set to 2 months, decide whether to change it so that next year's planning has more to work with.

Build the budget from what you can trust

Once you know which numbers hold up, split next year's spend by how much evidence sits behind it. This keeps the plan honest without freezing it.

Each line in the budget should say which group it is in and why. That is what lets you defend it, and what lets you move money later without starting the argument from scratch.

  • Proven: channels where spend, conversions and sales all line up. Fund these first, at a level you can justify from last year.
  • Unclear: channels that spend money but whose results you cannot yet verify. Keep them, fix the tracking early in the year, and review them by a set date.
  • Test: new channels or ideas. Give them a fixed amount, a clear question to answer and an end date.
  • Fixed costs: fees and tools. Review each one against what it actually delivers, not what it was bought to do.

Treat forecasts as a range, not a promise

Platform forecasts can help, as long as you know what they rest on. Google's Performance Planner lets you plan advertising spend and see projected monthly and quarterly performance. Google says its forecasts are refreshed daily and based on the last 7 to 10 days, adjusted for seasonality. It also says forecast conversions are based on the conversions you are already counting.

That last point matters. If your conversion tracking is counting the wrong things, the forecast will confidently project the wrong things. Use forecasts to test the shape of a plan, then sense check them against real sales.

Plan in ranges. A low, middle and high case for each main channel is more useful to a board than a single precise figure nobody believes.

Write down what would change the plan

A good budget says in advance what would make you move money. Agree a few simple rules before the year starts, and put a date in the diary to review them.

This turns the budget from a once a year guess into a plan that can learn. It also protects the team from moving money every time one week looks bad.

  • Which numbers you will review, and how often.
  • What result would earn a channel more budget, and what would lose it some.
  • Who decides, and how quickly money can move.
  • What you will fix first if the tracking still cannot be trusted.

If last year's numbers still do not add up after these checks, the gap is usually in tracking, reporting or how spend is split, not in the market. A marketing performance audit is built to find that gap, show where money was lost, and give you a baseline you can plan next year's budget against.
